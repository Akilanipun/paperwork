What this contract covers

Most freelance disputes are not about the quality of the work. They are about scope, timing and payment. So the template is built around four questions:

  • What exactly is being delivered? The scope clause lists the work line by line, with a separate list of exclusions. This is the single most useful page you will write all year.
  • When does money move? Fee, deposit, payment window and what happens if the invoice is ignored.
  • Who owns the output? Rights transfer on full payment, or the client owns it immediately with a portfolio licence back to you.
  • How does either side get out? Notice period, payment for work already done, and which clauses survive.

It also handles the unglamorous parts: client delays extending deadlines, deemed acceptance after five working days, confidentiality for two years, and a liability cap equal to the fees paid. Those clauses are what stop a small project turning into an open-ended obligation.

When a shorter document is enough

For a one-off job under a few hundred pounds, a clear email with the scope, price, deadline and payment date can be enough — and it is still a contract. Use this generator when the project runs over several weeks, involves a deposit, includes intellectual property the client will reuse, or when the client's procurement team asks for a signed agreement.

What to check before you send it

  1. Scope, in the client's words. If the client described the job in an email, reuse their phrasing. Ambiguity is what gets argued about later.
  2. Revisions. Say how many rounds are included. "Two rounds of revisions" prevents an infinite loop.
  3. Payment terms. Fourteen days is common for small clients; thirty is common for larger ones. Whatever you choose, put the date on the invoice too.
  4. Late payment. The statutory interest clause is a deterrent, not a threat. Keep it in unless the client objects.
  5. Deposit. If you take one, say whether it is refundable. Non-refundable once work starts is standard.
  6. Governing law. If you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland, change it. Cross-border work with a US client usually means agreeing one side's law explicitly.
Not legal advice

This is a general template written in plain English. It is not a substitute for advice from a solicitor, especially for property, employment, regulated industries or contracts above roughly £10,000.

Questions

Do I legally need a written contract?

A verbal agreement can still be a binding contract in the UK, but it is hard to prove what was agreed. A written contract records scope, price and payment terms — which is what most disputes are actually about. Many client procurement teams also will not raise a purchase order without one.

Can I charge interest on late payments?

Business-to-business invoices can carry statutory interest under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 — 8% above the Bank of England base rate, plus fixed compensation of £40, £70 or £100 depending on the debt. You need to be able to show your payment terms and that the invoice was received. Consumer debts work differently, so check before relying on it.

Who owns the work?

By default, the creator owns copyright. The contract should state when ownership passes — usually on full payment. Keeping the rights until you are paid is useful leverage, and it is a normal term that clients accept.

Can I sign it electronically?

Yes. In the UK, a typed name, a scanned signature or an e-signature service is generally acceptable for a commercial contract. What matters is evidence that both parties agreed to the same document, so keep the email chain or the signed copy.

What if the client sends their own contract?

Read it, and compare it against this one on scope, payment timing, IP and termination. Clients often have a liability cap and a payment-on-completion clause that shifts risk onto you. You can propose this document as a starting point, or ask for specific changes to theirs.
