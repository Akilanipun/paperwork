The order matters

Most of the costs people regret come from doing things out of sequence — paying for a registered agent before the name is available, or opening a bank account before the EIN arrives. The checklist follows the order that avoids rework:

  1. Name and state first, because everything else depends on them.
  2. Registered agent before filing, because most states require the agent's consent.
  3. Formation documents, then the operating agreement, then the EIN.
  4. Bank account last, once you have the filed certificate and the EIN.

The three costs people forget

Annual fees. Almost every state charges an annual or biennial report fee, and some — California and Texas among them — add a franchise tax or minimum payment. A cheap formation state can be expensive to maintain.

Foreign qualification. If you form in Delaware but live and work in Texas, you usually need to register as a foreign entity in Texas too, paying both sets of fees.

Commingling. Not a fee, but the most expensive mistake. Paying personal bills from the business account can let a court disregard the LLC and hold you personally liable.

LLC or sole proprietor?

For a one-person business with no meaningful liability exposure, a sole proprietorship with an EIN and a separate bank account is simpler and cheaper. An LLC starts to make sense when you sign contracts, hold client data, sell physical products, hire people, or want a name that is distinct from your own. It is liability protection and structure — not a tax saving, unless you elect S-corp treatment and pay yourself a reasonable salary.

State rules differ

Filing fees, publication requirements, annual reports and franchise taxes vary widely. Confirm the details with your state's Secretary of State before filing. This is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Questions

How much does it cost?

State filing fees run from roughly $40 to $500, plus annual report or franchise tax fees. A registered agent service is typically $50–$300 a year. The EIN is free from the IRS.

Do I need an operating agreement?

Most states do not require one for a single-member LLC, but banks and courts often expect it, and it specifies what happens if ownership changes. Multi-member LLCs should always have one.

Delaware or Wyoming?

Only with a reason. If you live and operate in one state, forming elsewhere usually means paying fees in both and registering as a foreign entity where you actually work. Delaware suits venture-backed startups; Wyoming has low annual fees and privacy — but neither replaces understanding your home state's rules.

When do I need an EIN?

To open a business bank account, hire employees and file most returns. Single-member LLCs without employees can sometimes use the owner's SSN, but an EIN keeps the SSN out of everyday paperwork and is free from the IRS.
